Innovation in History: Impact, Influence, Change

A Bicycle for the Mind

How Apple's innovations made technology personal

Thesis

Apple did not invent the personal computer, the mouse, the music player, or the smartphone. Its innovation was turning these complicated technologies into simple tools that ordinary people wanted to use. From the Apple II in 1977 to the iPhone in 2007, Apple moved computing out of offices and labs and into homes and pockets. This changed how billions of people work and communicate and influenced nearly every device made since, but it also raised questions about labor, privacy, and screen time that society is still debating.

Background · 1945–1976

Computers for the Few

The first electronic computers of the 1940s filled entire rooms. Through the 1960s, computers were expensive machines owned by governments, universities, and large companies such as IBM. Ordinary people never touched one. Programmers often fed in instructions on punched cards and waited hours for results.

The Hobbyist Revolution

In January 1975, the Altair 8800 appeared on the cover of Popular Electronics. For about $400, hobbyists could build their own computer from a kit. But the Altair had no keyboard or screen. Users programmed it by flipping switches and read the results in blinking lights.

Hobbyists in Silicon Valley formed the Homebrew Computer Club to trade ideas. One member, Steve Wozniak, designed his own computer. His friend Steve Jobs believed that people who were not hobbyists would want one too, if it were easy to use.

Innovation · 1976–2008

Making Technology Usable

Apple's major innovations followed a pattern: take an idea that already existed and redesign it so anyone could use it.

  1. 1977Apple II
  2. 1984Macintosh
  3. 2001iPod
  4. 2007iPhone

1976–1979

Apple II: A Computer in a Box

Jobs and Wozniak founded Apple Computer on April 1, 1976. Their first product, the Apple I, was a bare circuit board. The Apple II, released in 1977, arrived fully assembled in a plastic case with a keyboard and color graphics. Buyers could connect it to a TV and start working. When VisiCalc, the first spreadsheet program for personal computers, came out for it in 1979, businesses had a reason to buy one too.

1979–1984

Macintosh: Point and Click

In 1979, Jobs toured Xerox PARC, where researchers had built a computer controlled with a mouse and on-screen windows. Xerox never turned it into a popular product. Apple did. The Macintosh, launched in January 1984, let users click icons and menus instead of memorizing typed commands.

1997–2003

iPod and iTunes: Music Goes Digital

By 1997, Apple was losing money. Jobs, who had been forced out in 1985, returned to lead the company. In 2001, Apple introduced the iPod, a pocket-sized player that held 1,000 songs. In 2003, the iTunes Music Store began selling songs legally for 99 cents each, at a time when many people downloaded music illegally.

2007–2008

iPhone: The Internet in Your Pocket

Smartphones like the BlackBerry already existed, but they had tiny keyboards and clumsy web browsers. The iPhone, announced in January 2007, replaced the keyboard with a multi-touch screen and displayed full websites. In 2008, the App Store let outside developers sell software for it, turning one phone into a camera, a map, a game console, and more.

Impact & Influence

How the World Responded

Short-Term Impact

Apple's innovations had immediate effects. When Apple first sold stock to the public in 1980, it was the largest IPO since Ford's in 1956. In 1985, the Macintosh, Apple's LaserWriter printer, and PageMaker software let people design newsletters and flyers at a desk, creating desktop publishing. The iTunes Store sold one million songs in its first week and 25 billion by 2013.

Influence on Competitors

Rivals copied Apple's ideas. Microsoft released Windows in 1985, bringing a point-and-click design to most of the world's computers. When the iPhone appeared, Microsoft's CEO doubted it would succeed.

He was wrong. Google's Android soon adopted touchscreens and an app store, and in 2016 Apple sold its one-billionth iPhone. That same year, BlackBerry, once the leading smartphone maker, stopped designing its own phones.

Change · 2008–Today

A World Reshaped

Over time, Apple's innovations changed daily life. In 2011, 35 percent of American adults owned a smartphone. Today, about 91 percent do. People now use phones to navigate, pay for things, take photos, and do homework, jobs that once needed separate devices. New industries, from app development to ride-sharing, grew out of the smartphone.

Idea: interview a parent or grandparent about life before smartphones. An oral history interview counts as a primary source, and a short quote from it would fit right here.

The Costs of Change

These changes came with costs. In 2010, a series of worker suicides at Foxconn, the Chinese company that assembled iPhones, exposed long hours and harsh conditions. A 2012 Fair Labor Association investigation confirmed serious violations. More recently, psychologist Jonathan Haidt has argued that smartphones harm teenagers' mental health, and in 2024 the U.S. government sued Apple, claiming it uses the iPhone to block competition.

Conclusion

Technology for Everyone

Apple's greatest innovation was not one device. It was the idea that powerful technology should be simple enough for anyone to use. The Apple II, Macintosh, iPod, and iPhone each took a complicated invention and made it personal. Their impact reshaped the computer, music, and phone industries, and their influence appears in nearly every device people use today. The change is still unfolding as society weighs the benefits of constant connection against its costs.

Annotated Bibliography

This is a starter list in MLA format. Keep only sources you actually read or watched, check every detail against the real source, and rewrite each annotation to say how you used it and how it helped you understand the topic.

Primary Sources

  1. Apple Computer, Inc. “1984.” Directed by Ridley Scott, Chiat/Day, 22 Jan. 1984. Television commercial broadcast during Super Bowl XVIII.

    This one-minute commercial, inspired by George Orwell's novel Nineteen Eighty-Four, announced the Macintosh. It showed how Apple presented the Mac as a tool that would free ordinary people, and a clip of it appears on the Innovation page.

  2. Apple Computer, Inc. “Apple Launches the iTunes Music Store.” Apple Newsroom, 28 Apr. 2003.

    This press release announced the iTunes Music Store and its 99-cent price per song. It explained how Apple offered a legal alternative to illegal downloading.

  3. Apple Computer, Inc. “Apple Presents iPod.” Apple Newsroom, 23 Oct. 2001.

    Apple's announcement of the first iPod describes it as holding 1,000 songs. It provided the date and features used on the Innovation page.

  4. Apple Computer, Inc. “iTunes Music Store Sells Over One Million Songs in First Week.” Apple Newsroom, 5 May 2003.

    This release reported the store's first-week sales, which show how quickly people accepted paying for digital music.

  5. Apple Inc. “Apple Celebrates One Billion iPhones.” Apple Newsroom, 27 July 2016.

    Apple announced it had sold its one-billionth iPhone. This statistic shows the scale of the iPhone's impact nine years after its release.

  6. Apple Inc. “Apple Reinvents the Phone with iPhone.” Apple Newsroom, 9 Jan. 2007.

    The original iPhone press release lists its features, including the multi-touch screen and full web browser, and how Apple described the device at the time.

  7. Apple Inc. “iPhone App Store Downloads Top 10 Million in First Weekend.” Apple Newsroom, 14 July 2008.

    This release shows how quickly people began using apps, which turned the iPhone into a platform for other companies' software.

  8. Apple Inc. “iTunes Store Sets New Record with 25 Billion Songs Sold.” Apple Newsroom, 6 Feb. 2013.

    This milestone demonstrates the long-term impact of iTunes on how people bought music.

  9. Dell, Michael. Remarks at Gartner Symposium/ITxpo. 6 Oct. 1997, Orlando, FL.

    Dell's comment that he would shut Apple down, widely reported at the time, shows how close the company came to failure before Jobs returned.

  10. Fair Labor Association. Independent Investigation of Apple Supplier, Foxconn. Fair Labor Association, Mar. 2012.

    This investigation of the factories that built Apple products documented excessive overtime and other violations. It shows a hidden cost of Apple's success.

  11. Jobs, Steve. Interview. Memory & Imagination: New Pathways to the Library of Congress, directed by Julian Krainin and Michael R. Lawrence, Krainin Productions, 1990.

    In this documentary, Jobs describes the computer as “a bicycle for our minds.” The quote inspired the title of this website and summarizes Apple's goal.

  12. Jobs, Steve. Keynote address. Macworld Conference and Expo, 9 Jan. 2007, Moscone Center, San Francisco.

    In this presentation, Jobs introduced the iPhone. Watching it showed how Apple explained multi-touch to an audience used to keyboard phones, and a clip appears on the Innovation page.

  13. Lieberman, David. “CEO Forum: Microsoft's Ballmer Having a ‘Great Time.’” USA Today, 29 Apr. 2007.

    In this interview, Microsoft's CEO predicted the iPhone would fail. It shows how competitors underestimated Apple's innovation.

  14. Roberts, H. Edward, and William Yates. “Altair 8800: The Most Powerful Minicomputer Project Ever Presented.” Popular Electronics, Jan. 1975, pp. 33–38.

    This magazine article introduced the Altair kit to hobbyists. It shows what personal computing looked like before Apple, when users had to build and program machines themselves.

  15. United States, Department of Justice. “Justice Department Sues Apple for Monopolizing Smartphone Markets.” Office of Public Affairs, 21 Mar. 2024.

    This announcement explains the government's case against Apple and shows that Apple's influence is still debated today.

  16. Wozniak, Steve, with Gina Smith. iWoz: Computer Geek to Cult Icon. W. W. Norton, 2006.

    Wozniak's autobiography describes building the Apple I and Apple II and his time at the Homebrew Computer Club, in his own words.

Secondary Sources

  1. Computer History Museum. “Timeline of Computer History.” Computer History Museum, www.computerhistory.org/timeline/. Accessed day Month year.

    The museum's timeline gave background on early computers and the hobbyist era that came before Apple.

  2. Duhigg, Charles, and David Barboza. “In China, Human Costs Are Built Into an iPad.” The New York Times, 25 Jan. 2012.

    This investigation describes working conditions in factories that made Apple products and helped explain the costs discussed on the Change page.

  3. Freiberger, Paul, and Michael Swaine. Fire in the Valley: The Making of the Personal Computer. 2nd ed., McGraw-Hill, 2000.

    This history of the personal computer industry explains the Altair, the Homebrew Computer Club, and Apple's early years.

  4. Haidt, Jonathan. The Anxious Generation: How the Great Rewiring of Childhood Is Causing an Epidemic of Mental Illness. Penguin Press, 2024.

    Haidt argues that smartphones have harmed teenagers' mental health. His argument represents a modern criticism of the change smartphones caused.

  5. Hiltzik, Michael A. Dealers of Lightning: Xerox PARC and the Dawn of the Computer Age. HarperBusiness, 1999.

    This book explains what researchers at Xerox PARC invented and why Xerox did not sell it widely, supporting the idea that Apple innovated more than it invented.

  6. Isaacson, Walter. Steve Jobs. Simon & Schuster, 2011.

    This biography, based on interviews with Jobs, covers Apple's founding, Jobs's departure and return, and the development of the iPod and iPhone.

  7. Levy, Steven. Insanely Great: The Life and Times of Macintosh, the Computer That Changed Everything. Viking, 1994.

    Levy, a journalist who covered the Mac's launch, explains how it was designed and how people reacted to it.

  8. Levy, Steven. The Perfect Thing: How the iPod Shuffles Commerce, Culture, and Coolness. Simon & Schuster, 2006.

    This book describes how the iPod and iTunes changed the music industry and how people listened to music.

  9. Merchant, Brian. The One Device: The Secret History of the iPhone. Little, Brown and Company, 2017.

    Merchant traces the technologies the iPhone combined, such as touchscreens, and the workers who build it. It supports the argument that Apple's innovation was combining existing ideas.

  10. Pew Research Center. “Mobile Fact Sheet.” Pew Research Center, www.pewresearch.org/internet/fact-sheet/mobile/. Accessed day Month year.

    Pew's survey data on smartphone ownership provides the numbers used in the chart on the Change page.
